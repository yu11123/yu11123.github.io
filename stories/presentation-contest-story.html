6th NTU Presentation Contest / Silver Award / 8 December 2018

Finding the courage to tell my story.

Overview

I entered the contest hoping to become braver about speaking. In the final, I shared my experience of illness with 400 people, asking what gives us value beyond the work we can do.

The final-round speech

AI is here. Love is the answer.

AI 來了，但愛是解答

The title plays on AI and ài (愛), the Mandarin word for love. I used that wordplay to ask whether our value lies only in the work we can do, or also in the care we give one another.

AI and our value

Play from 00:43 ↗

The topic I want to talk about today is… Before I give you the answer, I’d like to tell you a story.

Does anyone know where this is? A breakfast shop. But this is the breakfast shop right outside my home. Every morning when I buy a sandwich, the woman who runs it gives me a warm welcome. And every morning, I chat with her while I wait.

Imagine that, in the future, a robot could take her place. It could recommend the breakfast that suits us best, make it immediately when we’re in a hurry, and switch languages depending on where a customer is from. This is where robots are heading.

Advances in computing power let robots do more, with capabilities related to deep learning and affective computing. Deep learning allows robots to analyze, distinguish, and learn on their own. Affective computing allows them to recognize, interpret, and even reproduce human emotions.

In the example I just gave, a robot could recognize me and immediately make many different breakfasts, perhaps even more nutritious and healthier than the ones the woman makes. That’s deep learning. It could also recommend my favorite breakfast based on my tone of voice, my habits, and what I usually like to eat. That’s affective computing.

With these capabilities, robots may replace highly repetitive, routine jobs within ten to fifteen years. Highly complex and creative work is what we currently consider harder to replace.

The thought of robots taking our jobs makes people afraid and uneasy. But why should we be afraid of robots? Is our ability to work the whole measure of our lives? What about our value as human beings?

Let me return to the question I asked at the beginning. The answer is actually AI, but this AI is a little different from the first one. Here, AI means ài, “love” in Mandarin. Love is the ability that sets us apart from robots. Even as robots enter more and more fields, our ability to work will never be the whole measure of our lives.

Illness and care

Play from 03:45 ↗

This thought began six years ago.

On September 20, 2012, when I was thirteen, I received a “gift” I’d never imagined receiving: a tumor four centimeters across.

As you can see, my wisdom tooth is inside the tumor shown here. Before that tooth had even emerged, the tumor had eaten away all its roots. Beside it is my molar. The tumor hadn’t eaten into it, but the pressure had made it grow upside down.

Because of this, I had two major operations, one six years ago and another at the beginning of this year. For half a year, I couldn’t eat properly. Imagine having to blend all your food into a paste before eating, force your mouth open, and then use a syringe to rinse the wound, because all the food would get into it.

Before that, I’d always believed that if I studied hard, I could get into a good school and find a good job. But when illness suddenly appeared, my first thought wasn’t that all my hard work had been wasted. It was this:

I haven’t properly told the people around me how much I love them. I haven’t even said goodbye, and now I’m going to leave.

During that time, I received more love and warmth than usual. My elementary-school choir teacher gave me a copy of Mayday’s album Second Round, with a letter:

I hope this CD brings warm, sunny May days into your life, and that all the clouds in your heart drift away.

Then I understood:

When I’ve lost everything, I still have love. Not only the love others give me, but also my own ability to love others.

And I survived. I stopped thinking only of myself and began giving my love back to the world. This “gift” led me into my second life.

Giving back

Play from 06:12 ↗

I became a volunteer at the counseling office of Taipei Zhongshan Girls High School, where I began learning about empathy and listening to other people’s stories.

I volunteered with The Big Issue, keeping older homeless vendors company as they sold magazines.

Through these stories, I gradually came to understand that something more serious than losing the ability to work is losing meaning, losing love.

Robots may replace many human jobs in the future. But the care and warmth people give one another have a value that robots cannot replace. And love is the reason we exist.

As WeiBird sang: “Because of love, I love. Through wind and sun, I came to understand: it was all because of love.”

Let’s embrace AI and care for the people around us.

Because when AI arrives, love is the answer.

I’m Chen Wen-Yu. Thank you.

人工智慧與人的價值

從 00:43 播放 ↗

我今天要說的題目是——在得到答案以前，我想先說一個故事。

有人知道這裡是哪裡嗎？早餐店。不過，這是我家門口的早餐店。每天早上去買三明治，我都可以得到阿姨熱情的招待；但我也每天一邊跟阿姨聊天，一邊等待。

想像一下，未來可能會有機器人代替阿姨為我們服務。它可以自動推薦最適合我們的早餐；在我們趕時間時馬上製作，不用等待；也能隨著客人的國籍不同，立刻轉換語言。這正是機器人未來的趨勢。

透過運算能力的進步，機器人可以做更多事情，而這種能力與深度學習及情感運算有關。深度學習讓機器人可以分析、分辨和自我學習；情感運算則讓機器人辨識、解讀，甚至複製人的情緒。

拿剛才的例子來說，機器人可以辨認我是誰，也可以立刻做出許多不同的早餐，可能還比阿姨做得更營養、更健康。這是深度學習。機器人也可以透過我的語氣、習慣，以及我平常喜歡吃什麼，自動推薦我最喜歡的早餐。這就是情感運算。

因為機器人未來將擁有這些能力，高重複性、高例行性的工作，可能在 10 到 15 年內被機器人取代；高複雜性、高創意性的工作，則是人類目前認為比較難被取代的。

也因為機器人將搶走人類的工作，人開始感到惶恐、不安。只是，我們為什麼要害怕機器人？工作能力就是我們人生的全部價值嗎？那麼，身為人的價值呢？

回到我開頭問題的答案。其實，這裡的答案是 AI，但這裡的 AI 跟前面的 AI 不太一樣。這裡的 AI 指的是「愛」。唯有愛，才是我們能夠和機器人區別出來的能力。即便未來機器人進入的領域不斷增加，工作能力也絕對不是我們人生的全部價值。

病痛與被愛的經驗

從 03:45 播放 ↗

這個想法是從六年前產生的。

2012 年 9 月 20 日，那年我 13 歲。我收到了一份這輩子從沒想過會得到的「禮物」：一顆四公分大的腫瘤。

大家可以看到，旁邊的腫瘤裡面是我的智齒。我的智齒還在身體裡的時候，牙根就全部被腫瘤吃掉了。旁邊的是我的臼齒，它雖然沒有被腫瘤吃掉，卻因為腫瘤壓迫而倒著長。

也因為這樣，我在六年前和今年初分別動了兩次大手術。我長達半年的時間沒有辦法好好吃東西。大家可以想像，在吃東西以前，都要先把食物全部打成糊，然後硬逼著自己把嘴巴張開，再拿著針筒沖洗傷口，因為所有的食物都會流進傷口裡。

在那以前，我一直認為，只要好好念書，我就可以考到好學校，然後找到好工作。可是，當疾病突然出現在我面前，出現在我腦海裡的，並不是先前的努力全都白費了，而是：

我還沒有好好跟我身邊的人說，我多愛他們。我還沒有好好說再見，然後我就要走了。

在這段時間，我獲得了比平常更多的愛和溫暖。其中，我國小合唱團的老師送了我一張五月天《第二人生》的專輯，並附上一封信：

希望藉著這張 CD，能夠為你帶來人生和煦晴朗的五月天，從此所有心靈上的陰霾都遠離。

然後我才明白：

當我失去一切時，我還有愛。不只是別人給我的愛，我也還有給別人愛的能力。

然後，我活了下來。我不再只為自己著想，我開始將我的愛回饋於這整個世界。這份「禮物」，引領我展開了我的第二人生。

從被愛走向行動

從 06:12 播放 ↗

我加入中山女高輔導室小義工，開始接觸同理心訓練，聆聽別人的故事。

我加入《大誌》雜誌的義工，陪伴無家者伯伯，賣著一本本的雜誌。

我逐漸從許多故事中明白：比失去工作的能力更嚴重的，是失去意義，是失去愛。

或許未來機器人將取代人類許多的工作，但是，來自人們的關心和溫暖，都是機器人無法取代的價值。而愛，才是我們存在的目的。

就像韋禮安曾經在歌詞中唱的：「因為愛，所以愛；讓我風吹和日曬；我才明白，一切都是因為愛。」

讓我們擁抱人工智慧，並去關愛身邊的人們吧。

因為，當 AI 來了，愛才是解答。

我是陳玟妤，謝謝大家。

Finding the courage to speak.

In high school, even talking to a shop assistant had taken courage. I loved singing and playing guitar, but onstage I could shake so much that I couldn’t perform as I’d rehearsed. I took a presentation course because I wanted that to change. During my first short talk, my hands still trembled uncontrollably.

I entered the contest for the chance to practice in front of 400 people. Reaching the final brought a different fear: telling them about my illness. Some evenings, I stopped halfway through a timed rehearsal because I couldn’t stop crying. I began to worry more about breaking down than about shaking.

The story I chose to keep.

At thirteen, I was diagnosed with a tumor. I underwent major surgeries in 2012 and 2018 and spent months unable to eat normally. Treatment was painful. Through it, my family, friends, and teachers cared for me.

After the second surgery, I wanted to be able to talk about what I’d been through. I kept the story in my speech, even when rehearsing it brought me to tears. It had taken six years to feel ready to share it.

The care I’d received reminded me that I could still receive love and give it to others. In the speech, I connected that thought to listening to people’s stories as a school counseling-office volunteer and volunteering with The Big Issue. It became my answer to a question about AI: our value as people extends beyond the work we can do.

Seven minutes in front of 400 people.

My instructor helped me prepare, and my design-thinking coach and teammates gave me another chance to rehearse by asking me to present our work. Friends listened, held my hands before the contest, and came to cheer me on.

On December 8, I finished the seven-minute speech without shaking, even though my voice caught when I spoke about my illness. I received the Silver Award. Afterwards, audience members wrote to tell me the story had brought them warmth and love. I’d entered hoping to become a little braver; their messages helped me see what sharing my story could mean to someone else.
